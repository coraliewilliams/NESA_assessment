# Task 2 — Mark Imputation

Imputes missing (`M`) exam marks in `data/marks.csv`. This README covers the
data structure, the exploratory findings, and the assumptions that motivate
the imputation design. See `IMPUTATION.md` (added alongside the imputation
code) for the algorithm, its formal statement, and the evaluation results.

**Data integrity note:** `data/marks.csv` is never modified or overwritten by
any script in this project. All derived artefacts (figures, the imputed CSV)
are written to `output/` under different filenames.

## Data structure

- 1000 students × 49 exam-paper columns, plus `student_id`.
- Each paper column is named `<SUBJECT>-<WEIGHT>` (e.g. `ENG-10`, `ENG-90`).
  `WEIGHT` is both the paper's weighting and its maximum mark. Papers within
  a subject sum their weights to 100 (confirmed for all 20 subjects, no
  exceptions).
- A cell is one of three distinct states:
  - **blank** — the student is not registered for that paper (77.6% of
    cells, 38,034 / 49,000);
  - **`M`** — the student was registered but the mark is missing, e.g. due
    to illness/misadventure (0.37% of cells, 181 / 49,000);
  - **numeric** — an observed mark (22.0% of cells, 10,785 / 49,000).

  These categories never overlap (verified programmatically) and must stay
  distinct: only `M` cells are imputed; blanks always remain blank.
- 5 subjects (`MAT`, `BIO`, `CHE`, `GEO`, `MED`) have only **one** paper, so
  a same-subject "other paper" predictor doesn't exist for them. 14 of the
  181 `M` cells fall in these subjects.
- `M` is concentrated in a small number of students: 945/1000 students have
  zero `M` cells; the remaining 55 have between 1 and 9.

## Assumptions

1. **Blank ≠ `M`.** Blanks are structural missingness (not registered) and
   are excluded from the imputation target entirely; only `M` is imputed.
   This is a data-generating fact confirmed above, not a modelling choice.

2. **Missingness mechanism for `M`.** Under Rubin's (1976) taxonomy
   (MCAR / MAR / MNAR):
   - Illness/misadventure absence is plausibly close to **MCAR** *with
     respect to the mark the student would have achieved on that specific
     paper* — the absence is not directly caused by what that mark would
     have been.
   - There is a real **MNAR risk**: if the same illness also depressed the
     student's performance on papers they *did* sit around the same time,
     any predictor built from those observed papers (same-subject or
     otherwise) will be biased downward relative to the student's
     counterfactual "healthy" mark. The dataset has no exam dates, so this
     cannot be detected or corrected for — it is recorded here as a
     limitation, not resolved.
   - We proceed under a working **MAR** assumption (missingness is
     predictable from the student's other observed marks), which is the
     standard condition required for principled imputation (Little &
     Rubin, 2019; van Buuren, 2018), with the MNAR caveat above noted
     explicitly in `IMPUTATION.md`.

3. **Proportion-of-maximum is a valid common scale.** Figure 2 shows every
   paper's observed marks are non-degenerate and roughly unimodal on `[0,1]`,
   with no boundary pile-up — so comparing/pooling papers of different
   maxima via `mark / weight` is reasonable.

4. **A logit transform is appropriate for modelling proportions**, rather
   than working on the raw proportion or raw mark scale. Marks out of a
   known maximum behave like binomial-type proportions, where variance is
   mean-dependent (McCullagh & Nelder, 1989); modelling on the logit scale
   stabilises this variance and — critically — makes it impossible for a
   back-transformed prediction to fall outside `[0,1]`, which raw-scale
   linear models cannot guarantee.

5. **Within-subject signal is the primary, reliable predictor.** Figure 3
   (top) shows consistent moderate-to-strong correlation (r ≈ 0.51–0.68)
   between a student's papers in the same subject, across every multi-paper
   subject in the dataset. This justifies using a student's own performance
   on other papers in the same subject as the leading predictor for `M`.

6. **Cross-subject signal is weak and should not be assumed to help.**
   Figure 3 (bottom) shows subject-mean correlations are weak, inconsistent
   in sign, and centred near zero. A single global "student ability" latent
   factor spanning all subjects is not well supported here. Any model that
   borrows strength across the *entire* dataset (e.g. a global matrix
   factorization) must be benchmarked against a same-subject-only baseline,
   not assumed to outperform it — this is tested explicitly in
   `IMPUTATION.md`.

7. **Single-paper subjects need a fallback.** For `MAT`, `BIO`, `CHE`,
   `GEO`, `MED`, no same-subject sibling paper exists, so the 14 `M` cells
   here fall back to a population-level statistic (paper mean/median) or a
   cross-subject student-effect model rather than a same-subject predictor.

8. **Sample sizes are moderate, not large.** Each paper has 190–267 observed
   students; per-subject-pair correlations (Figure 3, bottom) are computed
   on smaller overlapping subsets and carry non-trivial sampling
   uncertainty. Any conclusion drawn from a single weak/strong cross-subject
   correlation should be treated cautiously — discussed further as a
   limitation in `IMPUTATION.md`.
